## Section 5 — Red flags worth taking seriously

Tick any that came up. None of these are automatically disqualifying — but two or more in the same program is a signal.

```
Program A:
☐ Wouldn't answer cost questions clearly
☐ Pushed admission within 24 hours without clinical assessment
☐ Talked about success rates without explaining how they measure them
☐ Promised outcomes ("we'll cure you", "you'll never use again")
☐ Refused to continue MAT I'm already prescribed
☐ Discouraged me from asking about discharge planning
☐ Wouldn't let me speak to anyone clinical before admission
☐ No clear answer about what happens if I escalate medically
☐ Pressure to sign before reading

Program B:
[repeat]

Program C:
[repeat]

Program D:
[repeat]
```

**About "success rates":**
Treatment programs that quote success rates are usually defining "success" in ways that wouldn't pass clinical review (e.g. "completed program" — not "abstinent at 12 months"). If a program quotes a number, ask: _"how do you define success, who measures it, and how long after discharge do you measure?"_ Most cannot answer cleanly. That is information.

## Section 7 — A note on perfect

There is no perfect program. There is the program that is _open_, _affordable_, _willing to take you_, _clinically appropriate_, and _acceptable enough_ that you will stay past day 5.

The two most common ways this decision goes wrong:

1. **Overthinking.** Comparing 8 programs across 40 criteria while the person who needs care is decompensating. Pick the best fit available now, not the theoretical best.
2. **Underthinking.** Going with the first program someone recommended without checking whether the level of care fits.

The middle path is what this worksheet is for. Three to four programs, scored cleanly, decided quickly.
