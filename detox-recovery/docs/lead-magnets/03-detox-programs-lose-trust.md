## Guide (PDF body)

### Why this guide exists

Every detox program I have ever encountered as a patient — or heard described in detail by other patients — was staffed by people who care. The trust gap is almost never a staffing problem. It is a _systems_ problem, and most of the system was built when the dominant model of addiction was moral failure, not chronic illness.

The ten patterns below are the most common reasons patients leave treatment early, refuse to come back after a relapse, or actively warn others away from a program. Each is fixable. Most are cheap to fix. None require changing your clinical model.

If you read this and recognize three or more, that's worth a conversation — but the actionable thing isn't to email me. It's to talk to the patient who left AMA last week.

---

### 1. The intake call asks the wrong questions in the wrong order

**What happens:** The first ten minutes of contact are insurance verification, billing, and demographic data. Clinical questions come later, if at all.

**Why it loses trust:** The patient called you because they were ready, scared, or both. They needed to hear "you're in the right place" within the first 60 seconds. Instead, they heard "let me transfer you to billing." Most never call back.

**What to change:** Insurance and demographics can wait two minutes. The first 60 seconds is for _acknowledging that they called_, _naming what happens next_, and _offering one piece of immediate help_ (a callback time, a number to use if they're in danger, a confirmation that they don't need to know anything before walking in).

---

### 2. Intake paperwork is treated like a legal document, not a clinical one

**What happens:** The patient arrives in active withdrawal and is handed a clipboard with 14 pages of consents, releases, and questionnaires.

**Why it loses trust:** They cannot read 14 pages in active withdrawal. They can barely hold the pen. They sign without understanding, which means they didn't actually consent — and they know it.

**What to change:** Three pages, large type, in the first hour. The rest can wait until they're stabilized. If they can't sign yet, document that and try again in 24 hours. The legal exposure of "we waited" is less than the legal exposure of "we got a signature from someone in delirium."

---

### 3. The first 6 hours are a vacuum

**What happens:** Patient is admitted, vitals taken, room assigned, meds dispensed if applicable. Then nothing — no orientation, no schedule, no peer contact, no explanation of what's happening — for hours.

**Why it loses trust:** Those first hours are when the patient is deciding whether to leave AMA. They are also when withdrawal is loudest and the fear of "did I make a mistake coming here" is sharpest. Silence reads as abandonment.

**What to change:** A 5-minute orientation in the first hour. A second check-in at hour 3. A peer or alumnus visit at hour 6. The content matters less than the cadence. They need to know someone knows they are here.

---

### 4. Medication is delivered with no information

**What happens:** Staff hands the patient a paper cup with pills, says "take these," and walks away.

**Why it loses trust:** The patient is in a substance use program. They have been told for years that putting unknown substances in their body is the problem. Now you are handing them unknown substances and asking them not to ask. The cognitive dissonance is enormous.

**What to change:** Name the medication. Explain what it does. Name the side effects they might feel in the next two hours. Confirm consent. Two extra sentences. No new clinical risk.

---

### 5. Discharge planning starts at discharge

**What happens:** On day 5 or 7, someone hands the patient a list of outpatient programs, a follow-up appointment, and a sober-living referral. They leave at noon. The relapse rate at 30 days speaks for itself.

**Why it loses trust:** Patients can tell when a transition was planned and when it was assembled in the last 90 minutes. They feel the difference between "we have been preparing you for this" and "we have been preparing your bed for the next person."

**What to change:** Discharge planning starts day 1. Patients should know the names of their outpatient providers by mid-stay. They should have spoken to their sober living house manager before they leave. The first outpatient appointment should be scheduled before they pack their bag.

---

### 6. Family is treated as either an enemy or an asset, not a person

**What happens:** Family members are either kept entirely out of the loop ("HIPAA") or used instrumentally (drive the patient here, sign this, attend this family session). They are rarely treated as people who are themselves in crisis.

**Why it loses trust:** The patient watches their family be cycled through your system. When they go home, the family is part of the recovery environment — or part of the relapse one. How you treated them affects which.

**What to change:** A 30-minute family conversation in the first 72 hours. Not a clinical session. A "here's what to expect, here's what to do, here's what not to do, here's a number to call." It costs almost nothing and it changes the discharge environment.

---

### 7. "Failure to follow program" is documented as character

**What happens:** Patient leaves AMA, refuses a group, misses a session, or relapses. The chart says "non-compliant," "manipulative," "not ready," "lacks motivation."

**Why it loses trust:** Patients see their own charts eventually — via discharge summaries, primary care providers, or insurance disputes. Reading "non-compliant" tells them the program is more interested in protecting itself than in understanding what happened.

**What to change:** Replace character language with behavior + context. Not "manipulative" — _"asked for an early discharge after a phone call from spouse; behavior consistent with anxiety re: childcare; offered phone access and second conversation; declined."_ It takes 30 more seconds and it documents a real event instead of a moral verdict.

---

### 8. Relapse is treated as a return-to-zero event

**What happens:** Patient relapses. They are told to start over. They lose their bed, their sponsor's confidence, their sober living spot. Re-entry feels punitive.

**Why it loses trust:** Relapse is part of recovery in every chronic-illness model. Treating it as a starting-from-scratch event tells the patient that progress is contingent on never falling. They stop telling you when they fall.

**What to change:** Build a relapse protocol that is _neither_ an automatic discharge _nor_ an automatic continuation. The patient comes in, the team meets, the level of care is reassessed, and the patient knows that being honest about a relapse will not automatically cost them what they have built.

---

### 9. The physical environment signals what you actually think of patients

**What happens:** The waiting room is institutional. The bedrooms are bare. The hallways are loud. The food is hospital food. The bathrooms don't lock.

**Why it loses trust:** The patient is making a decision in their first 24 hours: _"Is this a place that thinks I am a person, or a place that thinks I am a problem?"_ They make that decision off the building before they make it off the program.

**What to change:** You don't need to remodel. You need to walk through your physical space as a new patient and ask one question: _what would make this 10% less institutional?_ A working bathroom lock. A non-fluorescent reading lamp. A snack drawer. Window blinds that actually close.

---

### 10. Aftercare is a referral, not a relationship

**What happens:** At 30 days post-discharge, the patient gets a "checking in!" call from someone they have never spoken to. At 60 days, nothing. At 90 days, a survey.

**Why it loses trust:** The patient learned during treatment that _connection_ was the thing that helped. Then connection ended on day 30. The implicit message: _connection is a billable service, not a relationship._

**What to change:** Aftercare is owned by a single named person who the patient met in treatment. The cadence matters less than the consistency. Three real conversations over 90 days will outperform thirty automated touchpoints.

---

### One thing to take away

The patient does not need your program to be perfect. They need it to be honest about where it isn't perfect, and to treat them like the kind of adult who can handle that information.

Most of the trust gap is not a clinical gap. It is an experience gap, and experience is something you can audit, redesign, and improve without changing your model of care.
