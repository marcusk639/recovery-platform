## Chapter 2 — The first 72 hours

**The decision framework for hour 0–3**

Three questions to answer in this window:

1. **Is anyone in Chapter 1 territory right now?** If yes — ER, today. Skip the rest.
2. **Are they asking for help, or are you the one asking for them?** Both are valid, but they shape what comes next.
3. **What is the actual barrier to the next step?** Money, fear, childcare, work, shame, denial, logistics. Each has a different fix.

**Hour 0–24 — what you're doing**

- Containment: keep them in a safe environment (their home, your home, a friend's couch — _not_ a car, not alone, not somewhere they can't get to help)
- Information gathering: substance, amounts, last use, prescriptions, allergies, past detoxes, current health conditions
- One phone call: a clinician, treatment program, or fit check
- Hydration and small food
- Phone charged
- Document: write down everything you observe with timestamps. You will not remember it later.

**Hour 24–48 — what you're deciding**

- Outpatient or inpatient
- Medical detox or non-medical
- Now or in 24 hours
- Which program
- Who pays
- Who drives

**Hour 48–72 — what you're handing off**

By hour 72, the goal is that someone clinical has eyes on the situation. That can mean:

- They are in a medical detox bed
- They have an appointment with an addiction medicine clinician
- They have started telehealth MAT (medication-assisted treatment)
- They are stable enough to wait for an outpatient intake next week

If by hour 72 _nothing clinical has happened_ and you are still the only person watching them — that is the moment to escalate. ER is not the only option. Urgent care, primary care, addiction medicine, tele-MAT, and crisis lines are also escalations.

## Chapter 3 — Choosing a level of care

The American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM) defines levels of care that most US programs map to. You don't need to memorize these, but you should know they exist so you can ask programs which level they offer.

| ASAM Level                    | What it is                                       | Who it's for                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Level 4 — Medical Detox**   | Inpatient hospital or hospital-equivalent        | Alcohol, benzo, barbiturate, GHB/phenibut withdrawal; medically unstable |
| **Level 3 — Residential**     | Live-in non-hospital treatment, 24/7 staff       | Past acute withdrawal; needs structured environment                      |
| **Level 2.5 — Partial Hosp.** | 20+ hours/week in program, sleep at home/housing | Past acute, needs heavy structure                                        |
| **Level 2.1 — IOP**           | 9–19 hours/week                                  | Stable, working a recovery plan                                          |
| **Level 1 — Outpatient**      | <9 hours/week, often weekly therapy + MAT        | Maintenance, longer-term recovery                                        |
| **Level 0.5 — Early Inter.**  | Education, brief intervention, peer support      | Pre-diagnosis or post-treatment                                          |

**Things to ask any program before they admit your person:**

1. Which ASAM level are you?
2. What's your medical coverage on-site? (24/7 RN? On-call MD? Just techs?)
3. What medications do you use for withdrawal management?
4. What happens if their condition escalates beyond what you can handle?
5. How long is the typical stay?
6. What is the discharge plan and when does it start?
7. What is your relapse policy?
8. What does the day look like?
9. Can family communicate during the stay? When?
10. What does it cost out of pocket and what do you bill insurance?

If a program won't answer these in plain language, that is information.

## Chapter 4 — Treatment options decoded

**Medical detox** is _not_ a treatment. It's stabilization. The point is to get the person through acute withdrawal safely. After detox, the actual treatment begins.

**Residential / inpatient rehab** is a 30/60/90-day live-in program. Outcomes are best when residential is followed by continued outpatient care, not when it's used as a standalone "fix."

**Partial hospitalization (PHP)** is full-day program, sleep elsewhere. It's a step-down from residential or step-up from IOP.

**Intensive outpatient (IOP)** is 9–19 hours/week of group + individual. Works well for stable people who can keep a job.

**Outpatient therapy** is weekly or biweekly sessions. Works as maintenance or post-treatment.

**Medication-assisted treatment (MAT)** uses medications like buprenorphine (Suboxone), methadone, or naltrexone to manage opioid use disorder. It is evidence-based, FDA-approved, and significantly reduces overdose death. It can be combined with any of the above. Stigma against MAT is widespread and usually wrong.

**Peer support / 12-step / SMART Recovery** is community-based, free or low-cost, and lifelong. It is not a substitute for medical treatment when medical treatment is needed. It is often a critical complement.

**What "rehab" usually means in common speech** is residential treatment. But residential is one option, not the only one, and is not automatically the best one for your person.

## Chapter 5 — Insurance basics for treatment

**The questions you actually need answered:**

1. What is the in-network coverage for substance use disorder treatment?
2. What is the out-of-network coverage?
3. What is the deductible and how much has been met?
4. What is the out-of-pocket maximum and how much has been met?
5. Is pre-authorization required for any level of care?
6. How many days of residential are covered per year?
7. Is MAT covered? Which medications?
8. Are intake and assessment billable separately?

**Call your insurance directly.** Programs will run a verification of benefits, but they have an incentive to be optimistic. Calling your insurance directly gets you uncolored information. Ask for "behavioral health" or "substance use disorder benefits."

**Single Case Agreements (SCAs)** are negotiated arrangements between an insurance company and an out-of-network provider when there's no in-network option that fits. Most families don't know these exist. Programs sometimes will help you ask for one.

**Parity laws (US):** the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act requires most insurers to cover SUD treatment at parity with medical care. If you're being denied something that would be covered for a comparable medical condition, that is appealable.

**Medicaid:** in most US states, Medicaid covers SUD treatment, including some MAT and residential care. Coverage varies dramatically by state. SAMHSA's treatment locator (`findtreatment.gov`) can filter by Medicaid acceptance.

**Cash-pay programs** range from $5,000 to $80,000+ for a 30-day residential stay. Higher cost does not correlate with better outcomes.

## Chapter 6 — Communication patterns that help vs. harm

(Expand the table from free guide section 4 into longer scenarios. Include three scripts:)

**Script 1 — When they're still in active use and won't talk about it**

> "I don't need to talk about it tonight. I just want you to know I'm not going anywhere, and when you want help, I will help you figure out what kind. That's all."

Repeat. Don't add. Don't argue. Walk away if escalating.

**Script 2 — When they're in withdrawal and lashing out**

> "I know this isn't you right now. I'm not leaving the house. I'm going to bring you water and then sit in the other room. Yell if you need me."

Do not absorb the attack. Do not return it. Hold the room.

**Script 3 — When they relapse after treatment**

> "I'm not surprised and I'm not angry. I'm scared, and I need us to talk about what changed. Not tonight. Tomorrow. Right now I need you to be safe."

The conversation about what to change can wait 12 hours. The safety part cannot.

## Chapter 7 — The relapse conversation

The first relapse after treatment is not a failure of treatment. It is information about what the next step needs to be.

Questions to work through (with them, or with a clinician, _not_ in the first 12 hours):

- What changed in the 72 hours before relapse?
- Was the recovery plan being followed or had it quietly collapsed?
- Are the right medications in place? Is dose appropriate?
- Is the support network active?
- Is sleep, food, work in order?
- Is something specific being avoided (a person, a feeling, a memory)?

**Do not** treat relapse as a moral event. **Do** treat it as a clinical event that requires a plan change.

If relapse is becoming a pattern (more than once in 30 days), that is a signal that the _level of care is wrong_, not that the person is broken.

## Chapter 8 — Boundaries vs. ultimatums

**Boundary:** a statement of what _you_ will do.
_"I will not provide rent money while you are using."_

**Ultimatum:** a demand that _they_ change, with a threatened consequence.
_"If you use one more time, I'm done."_

Boundaries hold. Ultimatums break, usually within a week, and every broken ultimatum trains the person that words are cheap.

**How to write boundaries that hold:**

1. Make them about your behavior, not theirs.
2. Make them specific and observable.
3. Make them sustainable. (A boundary you can't hold for a year is not a boundary.)
4. Tell them once. Do not re-litigate.
5. Have a plan for what you'll do when you're tempted to break your own boundary.

Examples that tend to hold:

- "I will pay your phone bill so we can stay in contact. I will not give you cash."
- "I will drive you to treatment. I will not drive you to get more."
- "I will visit at scheduled times. I will not show up unannounced to check on you."
- "I will be honest with you about what I'm seeing. I will not lie to your employer."

## Chapter 9 — Self-care for the supporter

[Expand free guide Section 6 into a chapter. Add:]

**Find your own community.** Al-Anon, Nar-Anon, SMART Friends & Family, and online peer groups exist because the people in them learned the hard way that this is not survivable alone.

**Therapy is for you too.** A therapist who works with families affected by SUD is one of the highest-leverage things you can do for the person you love — because it makes _you_ more sustainable.

**Money rules:**

- Do not lend money you can't afford to lose.
- Do not put treatment costs on credit cards you can't pay off in two years.
- Do not raid retirement.
- Treatment that bankrupts the supporter does not improve outcomes for the person in recovery.

**Time rules:**

- Schedule one thing per week that is yours and not contingent on their recovery.
- Tell one person each week how you actually are.
- Sleep 7 hours when possible. Trade off with another supporter.

**Information rules:**

- Pick one source of information and stop doomscrolling.
- Read the same thing twice before reading something new.
- If a piece of content makes you panic, it is not useful right now.

## Chapter 10 — The 90-day plan

A loose framework. Adjust to your situation.

**Days 0–7 — Stabilization**

- Get through acute withdrawal in the right setting
- Establish a single source of clinical truth (a doctor, a program, an MAT clinic)
- Communicate the bare minimum to work / school / family

**Days 7–30 — Structure**

- Outpatient or step-down level of care in place
- Daily routine: sleep, food, meeting/group, work or rest
- One conversation per week with a peer or community (12-step, SMART, peer recovery)
- Family check-ins at a fixed cadence

**Days 30–60 — Maintenance**

- Reduce reliance on any one provider; build redundancy
- First "real" social situation handled (work event, holiday, family thing)
- Test boundaries: which ones held, which ones didn't, what changes
- Identify next-90-day risks: anniversary, season, financial stress

**Days 60–90 — Reassess**

- What's working? What's quiet but actually drifting?
- Is the level of care still right?
- Is the support network still active?
- Is the supporter (you) still sustainable?
- What does day 91–180 look like?

If 90 days have passed and _nothing_ in your life has changed, that is a sign you may have absorbed too much. Adjust.
