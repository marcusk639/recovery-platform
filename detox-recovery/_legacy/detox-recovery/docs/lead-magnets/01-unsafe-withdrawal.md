### Why this guide exists

Most of what's written about withdrawal is either clinical (written for doctors) or vague (written for people who haven't been through it). This guide is for the moment when something has shifted and you're trying to figure out whether what you're feeling is "normal withdrawal" or "I need to get to a hospital."

This guide is not medical advice. It does not assess safety. What it does is help you recognize specific signs that mean you need a higher level of care than you can give yourself — and what to do next.

---

### Section 1 — Stop reading and get to emergency care if any of these are true

These are non-negotiable. If you check any one of these, stop using this guide and get to an emergency room or call 911. Don't wait for the next symptom. Don't try to "ride it out."

- You are withdrawing from **alcohol, benzodiazepines (Xanax, Valium, Klonopin, Ativan), barbiturates, GHB, GBL, or phenibut** — these withdrawals can cause seizures and need medical management.
- You have a **history of seizures**, withdrawal-related or not.
- You are **seeing, hearing, or feeling things that aren't there** (hallucinations).
- You feel **confused, disoriented, or can't track time or place**.
- You are **pregnant**.
- You are having **thoughts of suicide or self-harm**.
- You have **chest pain or pressure**.
- You have **fainted or lost consciousness** at any point.
- You can't keep water down and feel **severely dehydrated**.
- Your **heart is racing, pounding, or skipping in a way that scares you**, or your blood pressure is unstable.
- You are experiencing **severe psychiatric symptoms** — panic that won't stop, mania, paranoia.
- You are withdrawing from **multiple substances at once** (polysubstance withdrawal).

If any of these are happening, the emergency room is the right place. Tell them what you used, how much, and when you stopped. They have seen this before. They will not call the police. Their job in this moment is to keep you alive.

**US crisis numbers:**

- Emergency: **911**
- Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: **988** (call or text)
- SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, 24/7, treatment referrals)

### Section 2 — Signs you need to see a doctor today (not tomorrow)

These are less acute, but they mean today, not "when I feel better":

- Tremors that are getting worse, not better
- Vomiting that won't stop
- Sweating through clothes
- Sleep deprivation past 48 hours
- Anxiety that is escalating instead of fluctuating
- Pain (joint, muscle, head) that you can't manage with rest and hydration
- Inability to eat for more than 24 hours
- New symptoms appearing on day 2, 3, or 4 when day 1 was tolerable

What "see a doctor today" looks like:

- Urgent care clinic
- Primary care office (same-day visit)
- Tele-health (if you have access)
- An addiction medicine specialist
- A medical detox program

Be honest about what you used. They're not going to fix this if you tell them half the story.

### Section 3 — What "normal" withdrawal feels like (so you can tell when it's not)

[personalize — a paragraph or two on what the early hours/days felt like for you, without specific dose or substance claims. Examples: "It felt like the flu but louder." "I couldn't sit still and I couldn't sleep." "Every sound was an attack."]

Common withdrawal experiences vary by substance, but most people report some combination of:

- Sleep disruption (early waking, no sleep, hypersomnia)
- GI symptoms (nausea, diarrhea, no appetite)
- Anxiety and irritability
- Sweating, chills, runny nose
- Body aches
- Cravings that come in waves
- Emotional flatness or grief
- Inability to feel pleasure (anhedonia)

These are uncomfortable. They are not, on their own, the conditions in Section 1. If your experience matches Section 3 and not Section 1 — you're in the part of withdrawal where pacing, hydration, food, sleep, and connection matter more than emergency response.

That doesn't mean "tough it out alone." It means the kind of help you need is different.

### Section 4 — What to do when you can't think clearly enough to plan

When withdrawal is loud, your prefrontal cortex is offline. You're not going to make good decisions about treatment options at hour 14 of no sleep. Plan ahead of that moment, or plan with someone else.

**Pre-commit to a single phone number.**
Pick one person — a friend, a family member, a sponsor, a clinician — and write their number somewhere you'll see it. The agreement is: _if I'm not okay, I call this number first._ Not because they'll fix it. Because they'll help you make the next call.

**Pre-commit to a single threshold.**
"If [specific symptom] happens, I go to [specific place]." Write it down. Tape it to a wall. Decide while you're calm so you don't have to decide while you're not.

**Pre-stage the ER trip.**

- ID and insurance card in one place
- Phone charger
- A short list of what you've used (substance, last use, amounts, dates)
- A medication list (anything you're prescribed, OTC, supplements)
- A person who knows you're going

You can put this in a folder, a phone note, or a text to yourself. The point is that the work is done before you need it.
